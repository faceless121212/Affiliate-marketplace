# What a "top 10 on Amazon this week" article may and may not show

By the Nativness team · Published October 3, 2026 · Updated October 3, 2026 · 9 min read

*Nativness publishes this page. Nativness is a devnet prototype with simulated escrow and no live offers or payout data, nothing here is ranked by performance, and we earn nothing from the links on this page.*

You may show your own picks, your own reasons and plain links to Amazon. You may not type in a price, paste a star rating, copy a customer review or hide where a link goes. Prices, availability, ratings and images have to come from Amazon's own links or API. And readers must be told you get paid, right next to the recommendation.

## Key takeaways

- A price you typed by hand is the fastest way to break the rules, because Amazon allows prices only through its own links or API.
- Star ratings and customer reviews are off limits unless you got them through Amazon's API.
- The FTC says "affiliate link" alone may not tell readers you are paid, and wants the disclosure close to the recommendation.
- Google's reviews guidance asks a "best" claim to come with your reason and first-hand evidence.
- A weekly roundup built on your own testing and plain links needs none of the risky parts.

## How we chose and checked

We took a weekly roundup apart into the 12 things it usually carries. Then we looked for the rule on each in three places: Amazon's Associates Program Policies and Operating Agreement, the FTC's FAQ on its Endorsement Guides, and Google's guidance on writing reviews.

We opened four pages and used all four. The Amazon policies page showed April 14, 2026 as its last update. The agreement showed October 15, 2025. Google's page showed December 10, 2025. We left out forum threads and blog summaries of the rules, because they age badly and often contradict the source.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice. The FTC material is US guidance.

## Can you show the price?

Not one you typed. This is the rule that catches most roundups.

Amazon's policy says your site "may only show prices and availability if" Amazon serves the link that displays them, or you get the data through its Creators API or PA API. There is no third option for a number you copied on Monday morning.

Think about why. You publish "this week" on Monday. By Wednesday the price has moved. Your reader clicks, sees a different number and feels tricked. Amazon's fix is to keep you from printing a number you cannot keep fresh.

Even API data comes with strings. If you refresh it "less frequently than hourly", the policy requires a date and time stamp next to it. It also requires a disclaimer that begins: "Product prices and availability are accurate as of the date/time indicated and are subject to change."

So what do you write? Try "check the current price on Amazon" as the link text. It is honest on Monday and still honest on Friday.

## Can you show star ratings or quote customer reviews?

No, unless they came through the API. The wording leaves little room.

"You will not display or otherwise use any of our customer reviews or star ratings, in part or in whole, on your Site unless you have obtained a link to that customer review or star rating" through the Creators API or PA API.

Read "in part or in whole" twice. A screenshot of the stars counts. A line like "buyers rave about the battery" lifted from a review counts. A rating you typed from memory counts.

Here is the better play anyway. Google's guidance asks you to "discuss the benefits and drawbacks of something, based on your own original research". Your reader came for your opinion. Borrowed stars are not it.

## What about product images?

Use your own photos where you can. They are safer and they do more work.

Amazon's policy says you may not "store or cache" an image you got through the API. You may store a link to it "for up to 24 hours". That makes a saved copy of Amazon's product shot a problem in a post that stays up for months.

Your own photo has no such clock. It also answers Google's request for "evidence such as visuals, audio, or other links of your own experience". One slightly crooked photo of the thing on your desk proves you held it.

## How should the links look?

Plain, and obviously Amazon. Two rules cover it.

First, no shortener or button used "in a manner that makes it unclear that you are linking to an Amazon Site". Second, you may not "cloak, hide, spoof, or otherwise obscure" the address of the site carrying your links.

If your link text says "see it on Amazon", you have told the reader where they are going. That is the whole test.

Google adds a thought worth stealing: "Consider including links to multiple sellers to give the reader the option to purchase from their merchant of choice." A roundup that only ever points to one store looks like an ad. One that points to the brand's own shop as well looks like advice.

## Where does the disclosure go, and what must it say?

Near the picks, in words a stranger would understand. A footer line does not do the job.

Amazon's Operating Agreement requires this sentence on your site: "As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases." That satisfies Amazon. The FTC asks for more than a label.

Its FAQ says: "Consumers might not understand that 'affiliate link' means that the person placing the link is getting paid for purchases made through the link." It suggests plain words: "I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post." On placement, it says "the closer the disclosure is to your recommendation, the better".

So put one clear sentence above the list. Readers who know how you are paid can decide how much weight to give your number one.

## Can you call it "top 10 this week" at all?

Yes, if the list is yours and you say how you made it. The pages we opened set no rule on the phrase.

The trouble is what the headline implies. "Top 10 on Amazon" sounds like Amazon's ranking. If it is really your ten favorites, say so in the first line: "Ten things I tested this week, in the order I would buy them."

Google's guidance sets the bar for the word "best". Include "why you consider it the best, with first-hand supporting evidence". And give the list "enough useful content" to "stand on their own". Ten names and ten links is a link farm. Ten short verdicts is a roundup.

Mind the wording about Amazon too. The agreement says you will not suggest Amazon will "support, sponsor, or endorse you". "Amazon's top 10" crosses that line. "My top 10 from Amazon" does not.

## A fill-in template you can reuse

Copy this, then fill the brackets with your own words.

```
[Number] things I tested this week, ranked by [your rule]

I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post.
As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases.

How I picked: [what you tested, for how long, what you left out and why]

1. [Product name]
   Best for: [who]
   What I found: [two sentences from your own use]
   One drawback: [be specific]
   [Your own photo]
   See it on Amazon: [plain link]
   Also sold at: [second seller, if any]

(repeat for each pick)

Not in this post: prices, star ratings, customer reviews, stock status.
Last checked: [date]
```

Notice what is missing. No prices, no stars, no "only 3 left". Every one of those would need the API and a timestamp. The post is stronger without them, because everything left is yours.

## FAQ

**Can I write the price if I add "at the time of writing"?**
The policy we read does not offer that route. It allows prices only through Amazon's links or its API, with a timestamp when the data is not refreshed hourly.

**Is the Amazon sentence enough for the FTC?**
Do not assume so. The FTC's FAQ asks for a clear statement that you are paid, placed close to the recommendation. One plain sentence above the list covers both.

**Can I send the same roundup to my email list?**
Amazon's policy allows links in email when the reader opted in. It does not allow links in an ebook, printed material or a mailing.

**Do I need to test all ten products?**
No rule we opened says so. Google's guidance does ask for first-hand evidence when you call something the best, so say plainly which ones you used and which you only researched.

**What is the quickest fix for an old roundup?**
Remove typed prices, star ratings and copied review lines first. Then move the disclosure above the list and check that every link plainly says it goes to Amazon.
