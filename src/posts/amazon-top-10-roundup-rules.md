It's Monday. You've got ten products, ten links and a headline that says "Top 10 on Amazon this week". You paste in the prices, grab the star ratings, hit publish.

Two of those moves break Amazon's rules.

The good news: the safe version of that post is also the better one. Here's what the rules actually say, without the legal fog.

> **The short version.** Show your own picks, your own photos and your own opinions. Link plainly to Amazon. Never type a price, a star rating or a customer review by hand. And tell readers you get paid, right above the list.

## The cheat sheet

| In your roundup | Okay? | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| Your own ranking and verdicts | Yes | Say how you picked |
| Your own photos | Yes | None |
| A plain "See it on Amazon" link | Yes | No shorteners that hide where it goes |
| A price you typed in | **No** | Only through Amazon's links or API |
| "In stock" or "only 3 left" | **No** | Same rule as prices |
| Star ratings | **No** | Only through the API |
| Quotes from customer reviews | **No** | Only through the API |
| Saved copies of Amazon's product images | **No** | API images can't be stored |
| "15% off this week" | Careful | Delete it the moment the deal ends |
| The same post as an email | Yes | Only to people who opted in |
| The same post as a PDF or ebook | **No** | No links in ebooks or print |

Now the why behind the big ones.

## Don't type the price

This is the rule that catches most people.

Amazon's policy says your site can show prices and availability only two ways: Amazon serves the link that displays them, or you pull them through its API. A number you copied on Monday morning is neither.

It makes sense when you picture the reader. You publish on Monday. The price moves on Wednesday. Someone clicks on Friday, sees a different number and feels tricked. By you.

Even API prices come with homework. If you refresh them less than once an hour, you need a date and time stamp beside them, plus Amazon's disclaimer that prices can change.

**Do this instead:** make the link text "Check the current price on Amazon". It's true on Monday and still true on Friday.

## Leave the stars alone

Amazon's wording is blunt. You can't display its customer reviews or star ratings "in part or in whole" unless you got them through the API.

"In part or in whole" covers more than you'd think:

- A screenshot of the stars
- "4.7 out of 5" typed from memory
- "Buyers rave about the battery life", lifted from a review

Honestly, you don't need them. Your reader didn't come for Amazon's stars. They can see those on Amazon. They came for what **you** think.

Google says the same thing in its guidance on reviews. It asks for benefits and drawbacks "based on your own original research".

## Take your own photos

You can't store or cache an image you got through Amazon's API. You can keep a link to one for 24 hours. That's a problem for a post that stays up for months.

Your own photo has no clock on it. It also does a job no stock shot can: it proves you held the thing. One slightly crooked picture of the product on your desk beats a perfect white-background render.

## Make links look like links

Two rules, both simple:

1. **No disguises.** Don't use a link shortener or button that hides the fact you're sending people to Amazon.
2. **No cloaking.** Don't hide or mask the address of the page your links live on.

If your link says "See it on Amazon", you've passed.

One idea worth borrowing from Google: link to more than one seller. A list that only ever points to one store reads like an ad. Add the brand's own shop and it reads like advice.

## Say you get paid, where people will see it

Amazon requires one sentence on your site:

> As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases.

That keeps Amazon happy. The FTC wants a bit more, and it's reasonable. Its FAQ points out that readers might not understand what "affiliate link" even means. It suggests plain words, like "I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post."

And placement matters. In the FTC's words, "the closer the disclosure is to your recommendation, the better."

So: one clear sentence **above** the list. Not in the footer. Not on an "About" page nobody opens.

## So can you still call it "top 10"?

Yes, as long as it's obviously **your** top 10.

"Amazon's top 10" sounds like Amazon made the list, and your agreement says you won't imply Amazon sponsors or endorses you. "My top 10 from Amazon this week" is fine.

Google's guidance sets the bar for the word "best": say why, and back it with first-hand evidence. Ten names and ten links is a link farm. Ten short, honest verdicts is a roundup people come back for.

## Steal this template

```
10 things I tested this week, ranked by [your rule]

I get commissions for purchases made through links in this post.
As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases.

How I picked: [what you tested, for how long, what you skipped]

1. [Product name]
   Best for: [who]
   What I found: [two sentences from your own use]
   One drawback: [be specific]
   [Your own photo]
   See it on Amazon: [plain link]
   Also sold at: [second seller, if any]

Last checked: [date]
```

Notice what's missing: prices, stars, stock counts. Everything left on the page is yours. That's the whole trick.

## Quick questions

**Can I write the price if I add "at time of writing"?**
The policy doesn't offer that option. Prices go through Amazon's links or its API, or they don't go in.

**Is Amazon's sentence enough for the FTC?**
Don't count on it. Add a plain "I get paid if you buy through these links" above your list and you've covered both.

**Do I have to test all ten products?**
No rule we read says so. But say which ones you used and which you only researched. Readers can tell anyway.

**What's the fastest fix for an old roundup?**
Delete typed prices, star ratings and copied review lines. Move the disclosure above the list. Ten minutes, tops.
