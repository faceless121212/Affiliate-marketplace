If you want to promote affiliate offers, there are a LOT of opinions on how to do it.

Believe me, I looked. Start a blog. No, blogs are dead, do short video. No, build an email list. No, run ads. Everyone has a favorite, and everyone's favorite is the thing they happen to sell a course about.

So we did something simpler. We listed the twelve methods that keep coming up, and scored every one on the same four questions. No hype, no income promises. Just: how fast, how cheap, how hard, and how long does it keep working?

> **The short version.** If you already have an audience, start with a resource page. It's nearly free and takes an afternoon. If you're starting from zero, pick ONE of newsletter, short video or LinkedIn and give it six months.

## So you want to promote affiliate offers?

Quick refresher, in case you're new here.

An advertiser has a product. You tell people about it using your own tracking link. When someone buys, signs up or books a demo through that link, you get a commission.

That's it. The hard part isn't understanding it. The hard part is choosing **where** to show up, and then showing up for long enough to matter.

### How we scored each method

Every method gets four scores from 1 to 5. Higher is always better for you.

- **Setup speed.** How fast can you get your first piece of content out? 5 means today.
- **Cost to start.** 5 means nearly free. 1 means you'll need a real budget.
- **Ease.** 5 means a beginner can do it. 1 means you'll need a skill most people don't have yet.
- **Staying power.** 5 means the work keeps bringing people in for months. 1 means it's gone tomorrow.

Add them up and you get a score out of 20.

**One honest warning:** these scores are our judgment, not measured data. We have no earnings figures and we're not going to pretend we do. Use the scores to compare methods, not to predict what you'll make.

## 12 ways to promote affiliate offers

Ranked by score, highest first. Ties are in no special order.

### 1. Build a "tools I use" page

Do people already ask you what you use? What camera, what software, what course got you started?

Then you're sitting on the easiest affiliate page there is. A resource page is one page on your site, or one pinned post, listing the things you actually use, with a line on why.

It works because it answers a question people were already asking. You're not pushing anything. You're saving yourself from typing the same reply for the hundredth time.

**The catch:** it only works if someone's asking. With no audience, a resource page is a list nobody reads. In that case, pick another method first and come back.

```score
Setup speed: 5
Cost to start: 5
Ease: 5
Staying power: 3
```

#### How to get started

1. Write down the 5 to 10 products you'd recommend to a friend without thinking.
2. Check which of them have an affiliate program. Skip the ones you wouldn't use for free.
3. Publish the list with one honest sentence each, and say up top that you earn a commission.

### 2. Start an email newsletter

Social platforms change their rules. Search rankings move. Your email list is the one audience you can take with you.

A newsletter doesn't need to be fancy. One useful email a week on a topic you know is plenty. When a product fits the topic, you mention it.

The magic is trust. People who open your email every Tuesday have decided you're worth their time. A recommendation from you lands very differently from a banner ad.

**The catch:** some programs restrict email. Amazon's policy, for example, allows its links in email only when the reader opted in. Read the program's terms before your first send.

```score
Setup speed: 3
Cost to start: 4
Ease: 3
Staying power: 5
```

#### How to get started

1. Pick one narrow topic. "Tools for freelance designers" beats "productivity".
2. Set up a free plan with any email platform and a simple signup page.
3. Send five useful emails before you recommend anything.

### 3. Make short videos

TikTok, Reels, Shorts. One phone, one product, thirty seconds.

Short video is the fastest way to find out whether anyone cares. You can post today and know by tomorrow. And you don't need a following to get seen, which is rare.

The best ones don't look like ads. Show the thing working. Show the mistake you made before you found it. Show the before and after.

**The catch:** a short video lives for a day or two. You're on a treadmill, and the link usually has to sit in your profile, not in the video.

```score
Setup speed: 5
Cost to start: 5
Ease: 3
Staying power: 2
```

#### How to get started

1. Film three videos showing one product solving one problem.
2. Put a single link in your profile that leads to your recommendations.
3. Say out loud or on screen that you earn a commission. Don't hide it in hashtags.

### 4. Write on LinkedIn (for B2B and SaaS offers)

Promoting software, courses for professionals or anything a company would buy? Your buyers are already scrolling LinkedIn at their desks.

Here's why it's underrated. B2B programs can pay for a lead or a booked demo, not only a cut of a sale. And a thoughtful post about how you solved a work problem doesn't feel like selling.

Write about the problem. Mention the tool where it honestly fits.

**The catch:** it's your professional name on the line. Recommend something bad to your colleagues and you'll feel it at the next conference.

```score
Setup speed: 4
Cost to start: 5
Ease: 3
Staying power: 3
```

#### How to get started

1. List three work problems you've solved in the last year.
2. Write one post per problem: what broke, what you tried, what worked.
3. Add the tool and your disclosure at the end, not the start.

### 5. Write comparison posts on a niche blog

The classic, and still standing.

Somebody types "X vs Y" into a search engine. They're not browsing. They're about to buy and want one last opinion. A clear, honest comparison is exactly what they need.

A blog post can keep bringing readers for months after you publish it. That's the appeal: you do the work once.

**The catch:** it's slow. Search engines take their time. And Google's own guidance asks reviewers for first-hand evidence. Rewriting the sales page won't cut it.

```score
Setup speed: 2
Cost to start: 4
Ease: 3
Staying power: 5
```

#### How to get started

1. Pick two products you've actually used and that people confuse.
2. Write the comparison you wish you'd found: who each one is for, and one real drawback each.
3. Add your own screenshots or photos. They prove you were there.

### 6. Answer questions in communities

Reddit, Discord servers, Facebook groups, niche forums. People ask "what should I use for this?" every single day.

If you're the person who gives the detailed, helpful answer, people notice. Over time they click your profile, find your site, and join your list.

**The catch:** this one's easy to ruin. Most communities have rules about self-promotion, and members can smell a drive-by link from a mile away. Read the rules first. Help ten times before you link once.

```score
Setup speed: 4
Cost to start: 5
Ease: 3
Staying power: 2
```

#### How to get started

1. Join two communities where your audience hangs out, and read their rules.
2. Answer questions for two weeks with no links at all.
3. Put your recommendations on your own page, and link to that only where the rules allow.

### 7. Run a deals page

People love a discount. A page that collects current deals in one niche gives them a reason to come back.

It's simple to build and easy to understand. Brands often hand affiliates special codes, which gives you something nobody else has.

**The catch:** deals expire, and a page full of dead offers is worse than no page. Some programs also require you to take a promotion down the moment it ends. This is a maintenance job.

```score
Setup speed: 4
Cost to start: 4
Ease: 4
Staying power: 2
```

#### How to get started

1. Pick one niche you know. "Deals on design software" beats "deals on everything".
2. List only offers you've checked today, with the date beside each.
3. Put a reminder in your calendar to prune the page every week.

### 8. Make YouTube reviews and tutorials

Some products need to be seen. Software, gear, anything with a learning curve.

A good tutorial does two jobs at once. It helps people who already own the product, and it shows everyone else what they're missing. And a video can keep getting found long after you upload it.

**The catch:** it's real work. Filming, editing, thumbnails, titles. Your first ten videos will be rough, and that's normal.

```score
Setup speed: 2
Cost to start: 3
Ease: 2
Staying power: 5
```

#### How to get started

1. Record your screen or your desk while you do one useful thing with the product.
2. Keep it under ten minutes and cut the intro.
3. Say you earn a commission in the video, and put it in the description.

### 9. Start a podcast

A podcast is the long game. Half an hour in someone's ears every week builds the kind of trust a blog post can't.

When you recommend something on a show, it's your voice saying it. Listeners treat that like advice from a friend.

**The catch:** nobody can click a podcast. You need a short link people can remember, and a page to send them to. Growth is slow too.

```score
Setup speed: 2
Cost to start: 3
Ease: 3
Staying power: 4
```

#### How to get started

1. Plan six episodes on one narrow topic before you record the first.
2. Make one easy-to-say page for everything you mention on the show.
3. Read your own recommendations. Don't play canned ads.

### 10. Teach a free mini-course or workshop

Teach people to do something, and use the tool while you teach.

A five-day email course or a one-hour live workshop works well for software and education offers. By the end, people have seen the product solve a real problem. They don't need a pitch.

**The catch:** you have to be able to teach. And a workshop nobody shows up to is a rough afternoon, so you'll need some audience first.

```score
Setup speed: 2
Cost to start: 4
Ease: 2
Staying power: 4
```

#### How to get started

1. Pick one result you can get someone to in under a week.
2. Outline five short lessons, each ending with one small task.
3. Mention the tool only where the lesson needs it.

### 11. Build a free tool or template

A calculator. A spreadsheet template. A checklist people actually print.

Useful free things get shared, and they get linked to. If your tool solves the step right before someone needs the product, the recommendation feels natural.

**The catch:** you have to build it. That takes skill or money, and there's no guarantee anyone wants it. Test the idea with a simple spreadsheet before you build anything bigger.

```score
Setup speed: 1
Cost to start: 3
Ease: 1
Staying power: 5
```

#### How to get started

1. Find a calculation or checklist your audience does by hand.
2. Build the plainest version that works. A shared spreadsheet is fine.
3. Add one line under the result: "Here's what I use for the next step."

### 12. Run paid ads

Pay for clicks, send them to a page, hope the commission beats the bill.

It's the fastest method on this list. You can have traffic in an hour. It's also the only one where you can lose real money by Friday.

**The catch:** there are several. Programs can limit paid traffic, and some ban bidding on the brand's own name. Ad platforms have their own rules about affiliate pages. And if the offer stops paying, your ad spend is already gone.

```score
Setup speed: 5
Cost to start: 1
Ease: 1
Staying power: 2
```

#### How to get started

1. Read the program's terms on paid traffic before you spend anything.
2. Set a small, fixed test budget you can afford to lose completely.
3. Send clicks to your own helpful page, not straight to the offer.

## 3 quick tips for whichever one you pick

### Pick one and give it six months

The biggest mistake isn't choosing the wrong method. It's choosing four and quitting all of them in week three.

Pick one. Put it in your calendar. Judge it after six months, not six days.

### Promote things you'd recommend for free

Would you tell a friend about this product if there were no commission? If not, skip it.

Your audience forgives a recommendation that didn't suit them. They don't forgive one that was obviously for the money.

### Say you get paid, every time

In the US, the FTC expects it, and it's also just decent. Its advice is to say it plainly and put it close to the recommendation.

Something like "I earn a commission if you buy through this link" does the job. Readers respect it more than you'd think.

## Before you promote anything: will the program actually pay you?

Here's the part most "how to promote" guides skip.

You can do everything right, send real customers, and still wait months for your money. Or never see it. So before you spend a single evening on content, check four things:

- **What counts as a conversion?** A signup? A paid plan that survives the trial? Get the exact wording.
- **When do they pay, and is there a minimum?** Some programs hold commissions until you pass a threshold.
- **Which traffic is allowed?** Email, paid ads and communities are the usual restrictions.
- **Can the advertiser afford it?** A great commission rate means nothing if the budget has run dry.

## Promoting affiliate offers: FAQs

### Do I need a website?

No. Short video, LinkedIn and communities all work without one. But you'll want a page of your own eventually, because it's the one place where you make the rules.

### Which method is best for a complete beginner?

By our scores, short video or LinkedIn. Both are free, both let you start today, and both tell you quickly whether your topic has an audience.

### How many offers should I promote at once?

Fewer than you think. Two or three products you know well beat twenty you've only read about.

### Can I use more than one method?

Yes, once the first one is working. A newsletter pairs well with almost everything, because it turns one-time visitors into people you can reach again.

## Final thoughts

Twelve methods, and not one of them is a shortcut.

The ones that score highest are the boring ones: a list of things you use, an email every week, a short video every day. They work because trust builds slowly and then all at once.

So pick the one that fits the audience you have, or the one you'd enjoy doing on a tired Wednesday. Then start before you feel ready. The plan matters less than the six months.
